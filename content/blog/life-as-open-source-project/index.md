I have been thinking about a simple question lately: why do human beings need stories?

I do not mean stories in the narrow sense of novels or films. I mean the stories we use to understand our own lives.

The more I think about it, the more the world feels like a machine that keeps generating stories. It keeps pushing uncertain things in front of us. We become anxious, choose, act, fail, adjust, and then look back. After something has happened, we arrange the mess into a version that makes sense. That version may be what we call meaning.

This essay is not an attempt to give anyone else an answer. I do not have one.

It is closer to a worldview note written for myself. I have realized that introducing what I do, where I work, or what I study is not enough. Those are identities and labels. What really shapes a life is how a person sees the world, how they understand time, and how they choose to respond to uncertainty.

So I want to write down why I am interested in research, entrepreneurship, AI, medical translation, writing, and personal growth at the same time. I want to explain why I do not want to be defined by only one identity. And I want to be clear about why I do not think life should only mean walking through a script someone else has already written.

## 1. The World Feels Like a Story Machine

I now have a strong feeling that the world is a story machine.

That may sound abstract, but the idea is simple.

If something is completely certain, it is hard for it to become a story. If you already know exactly what will happen tomorrow, what your life will look like ten years from now, and what every choice will produce, life may feel safer. But it loses a lot of tension.

If everything is completely random, that is not much of a story either. A story needs at least some structure. It needs us to believe that action and consequence have some relationship.

What gives life a story-like quality is the distance between certainty and uncertainty.

We do not know the future, so we fear it and hope for it. We try to turn the unknown into the known. We try to turn an uncertain future into an already-lived past. Then, once something has happened, we look back and explain it: that failure mattered, that choice changed me, that painful period helped make me who I am now.

Strictly speaking, those explanations may not be fully objective.

But people do not live only inside objective facts. We also live inside the stories we tell ourselves about those facts.

Often, meaning is not sitting there at the moment something happens. It is something we assemble when we look back.

That is also how I understand time. The future arrives as uncertainty. The present asks for action. The past becomes material for interpretation. A life is partly the process of turning unknown time into lived time, and then turning lived time into meaning.

## 2. People Want Certainty, But Not Only Certainty

People often say that human beings seek certainty.

There is truth in that. In a Chinese context, many families hope their children can pass civil-service exams, enter a stable system, and secure a predictable path. Stability means lower risk, more predictable income, and a life route that is easier for other people to understand.

But I no longer think the matter is that simple.

The pursuit of certainty is often uncertain in itself.

Someone preparing for a civil-service exam does not know whether they will succeed. They compete, wait, worry, and risk failure. That process contains pressure, ambition, suspense, and the possibility of a turning point. In other words, it is already a strong story.

But once the person really reaches the stable destination, the old uncertainty has been solved, and a new problem may appear.

After the main conflict disappears, will life start to feel too certain? Will the person begin to long for a new kind of change?

This reminds me of the feeling in Fortress Besieged: people outside the city want to get in, and people inside the city want to get out.

When someone is outside, the city represents stability, safety, identity, and belonging. When someone is inside, the outside begins to represent freedom, possibility, and a new beginning. Perhaps the city itself does not contain a final answer. Perhaps people simply begin to imagine that another life may still hold another story.

So I now think people are not simply seeking certainty, and they are not simply seeking uncertainty either.

People seek a bearable form of uncertainty.

Too much uncertainty breaks people.

Too much certainty can suffocate them.

The most alive state may be one where there is enough safety to stand on, but still enough unknown territory to choose, change, fight for something, and keep becoming.

## 3. I Do Not Want to Romanticize Risk

I do not want this essay to become one of those speeches about how everyone should take risks and how a stable life has no meaning.

That is not true, and it is not fair.

Some people have lived with long-term insecurity. Some people carry heavy family pressure. Some people have very few resources. For them, stability is not cowardice. It is a real need. Wanting certainty, wanting a place to breathe, is completely reasonable.

I am more willing to explore uncertainty partly because I have been lucky.

My upbringing was relatively good. My parents gave me a lot of support. I received a good education and encountered good platforms and opportunities. Many times, I feel that I have received more than my own ability alone should have earned.

Because of that, I do not want to package my choices as a superior philosophy of life.

If I am willing to take risks, it is not simply because I am braver. It is also because I have conditions that allow me to bear some risk.

But that is exactly why I feel this luck should not only be used to buy myself a more comfortable and safer life.

If I have received more than I deserved, then at the very least I should try to convert it into something more valuable.

Luck should not become only a source of superiority. For me, it should become a source of responsibility.

## 4. Scientific Training Gave Me Tools, Not Meaning

My background is in engineering and research.

Scientific training has shaped me deeply. It taught me how to observe facts, ask questions, design experiments, analyze data, build chains of evidence, and move toward more reliable conclusions.

These are important tools.

But I increasingly feel that scientific training mainly gave me tools. It did not automatically give me meaning.

It can teach me how to solve a problem. It does not always tell me which problem is worth spending years on.

It can teach me how to write papers, run experiments, and prove that something works. It does not necessarily tell me why I should do this, where I want to use my abilities, or what I want to create.

This has become stronger in me over the past few years.

A person can publish papers, complete projects, and keep moving forward inside a professional system. That does not automatically mean they know what they are living for.

I am not saying research has no meaning. On the contrary, I am grateful for the thinking habits research gave me.

But I do not want to stop at knowledge production.

What I want more and more is to connect science, engineering, medical need, AI, products, and business paths. In other words, I hope not to be only a researcher. I also want to slowly become a builder.

I want to know whether things from the laboratory can truly enter the real world. Can they improve real people's lives? Can they become more than papers, and turn into useful products, tools, methods, or systems?

## 5. What I Currently Mean by Real Value

If you ask me what real value is, I do not have a fixed answer.

Different eras have different technologies and different social problems. What feels important now may change in the future. Today, AI, medical devices, personalized manufacturing, and regenerative medicine feel important. Tomorrow, new problems and new tools may appear.

So I do not want to define real value too rigidly.

But if I have one direction that feels relatively clear at the moment, it is this: help more people receive better medical care and live with a better quality of life.

That is why I am interested in materials, 3D printing, biomedicine, medical translation, AI tools, and entrepreneurship.

On the surface, these interests look scattered. Underneath, they point to the same question: how can science and technology enter the real world and help real people?

I cannot say yet what exact form this will take.

It may be medical devices. It may be AI tools for research. It may be a personalized manufacturing platform. It may be knowledge communication. It may be a direction I have not imagined yet.

That uncertainty is acceptable.

Meaning is not always fully clear at the beginning. Often, people discover what they truly care about through action, collision, failure, and revision.

What I have right now is not a final answer. It is a direction.

## 6. Who I Hope This Writing Helps

I have also thought about what kind of reader I hope this blog can help.

Probably not everyone.

Some people know clearly what they want, and they live steadily inside a value system that works for them. That is good. I do not think they need to be changed.

The people I most want to speak to are those who have begun to doubt the default life but have not yet built their own story.

They may once have believed that if they studied hard, found a respectable job, and received social recognition, life would naturally become meaningful. Later, they slowly discovered that it was not so simple.

They can no longer fully believe the old story, but the new story has not grown yet.

That stage can be painful.

The old story has stopped working. The new story has not formed.

I understand that state because I am often in it myself.

I cannot give other people answers, and I do not want to pretend that I have understood everything. No one can truly answer for another person how they should live.

What I can do is put myself out there as a reference case.

How I feel lost, how I choose, how I make mistakes, how I adjust, and how I put scientific training, luck, responsibility, creative desire, and real pressure into the same frame. Perhaps that process itself can be useful to someone.

I do not want to give an answer.

I want to offer an example.

## 7. Life as an Open-Source Project

I like using GitHub as a way to think about this.

An open-source project is not a perfect answer. It is something that can be seen, modified, forked, and developed further.

I hope my life story can have a little of that quality.

Not because other people should copy me. Rather, if someone sees part of my exploration and finds it useful, they can take that part, modify it, rewrite it, and make it their own version.

I do not want to be a person standing above others and telling them how to live.

I would rather be someone still on the road, making my experiment logs public.

Which ideas later turn out to be immature, which directions fail halfway, which choices bring me closer to real value, and which things are only temporary self-romance. These can all be recorded over time.

That is also why I want to write a blog.

For me, a blog is not simply content output. It is a long-term process of self-organization. It turns vague thoughts in my head into language. It also lets my worldview be revised through repeated expression.

If, in the future, someone can use these writings to find a little more direction for themselves, then this work will feel meaningful.

## 8. What I Resist Most Is Being Defined

What I resist most is not one particular way of life.

I am not against civil-service exams. I am not against stability. I am not against traditional paths.

What I resist is this: a person never seriously asks what they want, and simply walks into a story written by other people.

Other people say what counts as success, so they pursue it. Other people say what counts as respectability, so they believe it. Other people say what the correct life path is, so they follow it.

I do not want that.

I can accept training, but I do not want to accept being defined.

I do not want to be defined only by education, discipline, research identity, academic evaluation systems, money, job titles, or external recognition.

Nietzsche said something close to "become who you are." I do not want to turn that into a slogan. To me, becoming myself is not about being willful or trying to appear special.

It is more like a responsibility.

I need to decide what problems I am willing to serve, what risks I am willing to take, what value I want to create, and what kind of person I want to become.

Other people can give me tools, training, and advice.

But in the end, no one else can live my life for me.

## 9. Creating as a Response to Uncertainty

When I put these thoughts together, the closest answer I have is this: respond to uncertainty through creation.

The world will not give me the answer in advance.

The future will not become fully certain.

I do not know whether the things I am exploring now will succeed. I do not know what kind of person I will become.

But if I refuse to act because I do not know the result, the story never begins.

Accepting uncertainty and creating my own story are not contradictions.

Accepting uncertainty means admitting that the world will not provide a standard answer according to my expectations. Creating my own story means choosing to act even without that standard answer.

I want to turn research into real value.

I want more people to receive better medical care and live with a better quality of life.

I want to convert luck into responsibility, scientific training into tools, confusion into exploration, and a personal story into an open reference case.

These goals are still incomplete. They are not fully clear. But they form a direction.

## 10. I Do Not Have the Answer, So I Choose to Create

I have not fully figured out how I should live my life.

I still feel anxious, hesitant, and uncertain. I still move between research, entrepreneurship, writing, practical pressure, and future possibilities.

Maybe that is exactly why I want to write.

Writing lets me see what I am actually thinking. It also lets immature thoughts slowly become a system that can be revised.

I do not want to wait until everything is certain before I begin.

Many important things in life cannot wait for perfect certainty.

Right now, the direction I can name is this:

I want to use science to understand the world, creation to participate in the world, and value creation to respond to uncertainty.

I want to treat life as an open-source project: constantly updating, constantly revising, constantly exposing problems, and constantly generating new possibilities.

I do not know where this story will eventually go.

That is exactly why it is worth writing seriously.
