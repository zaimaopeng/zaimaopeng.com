We have spent the past two years talking about AI as an information revolution.

That framing is not wrong. It is just too small.

Yes, AI helps people search faster, summarize faster, generate faster, learn faster, and decide faster. But I increasingly think the most important industrial shift is happening one layer deeper.

AI is not just making information easier to access.

It is starting to turn **expert skill into infrastructure**.

By that I mean something very specific: forms of judgment that used to live inside a small number of highly capable individuals — and sometimes inside unusually strong teams — are beginning to be standardized, packaged, distributed, and amplified by systems.

That is a much bigger change than "better access to knowledge." It changes the supply constraint of entire industries.

For a long time, the upper bound of many industries has been set not only by capital, machines, or data, but by something much more stubborn: how many top people exist, how slowly they can be trained, and how much complex work they can personally carry.

AI does not erase that bottleneck. But for the first time, it looks like part of it can be transferred into systems.

If that is true, then the real AI race is not only a race for better models.

It is a race to capture, structure, validate, and scale high-value workflows that used to depend on scarce experts.

---

## Information Was Never the Rarest Thing

The optimistic story around AI often sounds like this: once everyone has access to the same models, knowledge becomes democratized, and capability spreads naturally.

I do not buy that story in its simple form.

Information has never been the rarest ingredient in elite work.

The scarce part is usually something else:

- knowing which information matters,
- knowing how to reframe a vague problem into an actionable one,
- knowing how to reason under incomplete evidence,
- knowing which trade-offs are tolerable and which are fatal,
- knowing how to turn a judgment into a result that survives contact with reality.

That is why two people with access to the same tools can produce completely different outcomes.

One gets a plausible-looking answer. The other gets something that actually works.

The gap is not only knowledge. It is structured capability.

This is why I increasingly think of AI not primarily as an information tool, but as a **skill amplification and skill replication system**.

---

## What Makes a Skill "Scalable"?

Not every skill can be scaled. Not every expert workflow can be captured. But many high-value capabilities have a repeated internal structure.

A top expert usually does not succeed by intuition alone. Their intuition is often compressed structure.

They reliably:

- identify the real problem beneath the obvious one,
- separate constraints from noise,
- decompose work into executable stages,
- notice failure modes early,
- apply heuristics developed through experience,
- validate the output against reality rather than rhetoric,
- and iterate until the result is usable.

When that structure becomes visible, AI starts to matter in a different way.

The value is no longer just that a model can answer a question. The value is that a workflow that used to require one unusually capable person can begin to be represented as a sequence of reusable reasoning steps, validation loops, and decision frameworks.

That is the first hint that a skill is becoming scalable.

## Additive Manufacturing Exposes the Pattern Clearly

One reason I keep returning to additive manufacturing is that the bottleneck is unusually visible there.

People often talk about additive manufacturing as if the hard part is the printer, the material, or the process window. Those things matter. But in high-value, customized applications, the deeper bottleneck is frequently the expert workflow around the manufacturing process.

If you work on patient-specific implants, complex lightweight structures, aerospace components, or other high-stakes custom parts, the real challenge is rarely just "can we print it?"

The challenge starts much earlier.

### Stage One: Demand Translation

The application side does not give you engineering inputs.

A hospital does not say: please produce a geometry with this boundary condition, this porosity target, this performance window, and this validated manufacturing pathway.

It gives you a case. Anatomy. Risk. Recovery goals. Surgical constraints. Regulatory concerns.

Likewise in aerospace, the input is often a service environment, a mass target, a fatigue expectation, a reliability requirement, a cost boundary.

Someone has to translate this into engineering language:

- what performance actually matters,
- what constraints are hard,
- what can be traded off,
- what geometry is viable,
- what process boundaries are acceptable,
- what counts as success.

That is not data entry. It is expert judgment.

### Stage Two: Engineering Realization

Even once the engineering inputs are clear, the answer still does not fall out automatically.

Experts still need to reason through:

- material selection,
- structural strategy,
- process route,
- parameter window,
- manufacturability risk,
- validation burden,
- and the trade-offs between all of the above.

This is one reason additive manufacturing remains difficult to scale in many high-value use cases.

The issue is not only that the output is customized. It is that the path to the output depends on repeated high-level judgment across multiple stages.

That makes the workflow expensive, hard to replicate, and difficult to industrialize.

---

## Why Simulation Is Not the Full Answer

A natural response is: this is exactly what simulation is for.

Simulation is indispensable. But it is strongest when the model is known, assumptions are defined, and the boundaries of the problem are already legible.

It answers a very important question:

**Given this model and these assumptions, what happens?**

But many real-world engineering problems start before that point.

They begin when:

- the requirements are still fuzzy,
- the constraints conflict,
- the mechanism is only partially understood,
- the process still contains tacit adjustments,
- and the final solution has to be shaped under uncertainty.

In those cases, the rare thing is not just predictive computation. It is cross-layer reasoning.

This is exactly where AI becomes strategically interesting.

Not because it replaces physics, and not because it magically removes the need for experts, but because it can begin to make portions of expert reasoning more explicit, more structured, and more reusable.

If that happens, then high-end customized manufacturing can begin to move from **craft-based customization** toward **skill-standardized scalable customization**.

That is a very different kind of industrial promise.

## Software Is Already Running This Experiment

The reason I think this pattern matters far beyond manufacturing is simple: software is already much farther down this path.

Look at the best CLI-based agents today.

What they are doing is not just code autocomplete.

At their most interesting, they are starting to scale pieces of how top engineers — and in some cases, top engineering teams — actually work.

A strong software engineer does not add value merely by typing faster. The real value lies in things like:

- reading an ambiguous request and identifying the real task,
- building context quickly inside an unfamiliar repository,
- decomposing large work into safe executable steps,
- predicting where regressions will happen,
- deciding what to test and how deeply,
- balancing implementation speed against maintainability,
- iterating based on tool feedback,
- and producing a result that can actually ship.

The most interesting agents increasingly operate inside that workflow rather than outside it.

They:

- receive a task,
- inspect the codebase,
- form a plan,
- edit files,
- run tests,
- interpret failures,
- revise the approach,
- and return a deliverable rather than just a suggestion.

Once you add review loops, context management, validation tooling, and multi-agent coordination, you are no longer watching "AI write code."

You are watching the early stages of **engineering workflow replication**.

That is why software matters so much here. It is already demonstrating that AI can scale not only knowledge, but the repeated execution patterns of top practitioners.

In some contexts, it is beginning to scale not only an individual engineer's working style, but something closer to a competent small team.

## The Real Competitive Shift

Once you see the pattern, a different theory of competition begins to emerge.

We usually talk about industrial advantage in terms of:

- capital,
- equipment,
- distribution,
- data,
- brand,
- and talent.

Those still matter.

But AI introduces a new variable that may become decisive in many domains:

**who can best extract, structure, validate, and distribute high-value expert workflows.**

That is not the same as having the smartest people.

It is the ability to do something more difficult:

- identify what the smartest people are actually doing,
- separate signal from charisma,
- convert tacit excellence into a workflow,
- connect that workflow to evidence and verification,
- and make it usable by a wider organization.

If this works, then organizational growth no longer depends only on hiring more rare people.

It depends on how effectively rare skill can be converted into reusable infrastructure.

That is an enormous shift.

## Skill Infrastructure May Become the Real Moat

This is the phrase I keep returning to: **skill infrastructure**.

Not just models. Not just prompts. Not just agents.

Skill infrastructure.

By that I mean the set of systems that can carry repeated high-value judgment:

- workflow scaffolds,
- decision logic,
- validation routines,
- context management,
- exception handling,
- collaboration patterns,
- and feedback loops that allow skill to improve rather than decay.

In manufacturing, that may mean embedding expert design and process reasoning into the chain between demand, engineering input, realization, and verification.

In software, it may mean encoding planning, implementation, testing, review, and iteration into agentic CLI workflows.

In law, medicine, education, consulting, research, and finance, I suspect parallel structures will emerge in different forms.

The outward tools will vary. The internal logic will rhyme.

A complex capability that once depended on a few people will gradually become something a system can partially carry, repeatedly execute, and continuously improve.

That is the kind of change that creates real industry discontinuity.

---

## This Is Exciting — and Unsettling

There is an optimistic version of this story and an unsettling one.

The optimistic version is obvious.

If high-level capability becomes more scalable, then sectors limited by expert scarcity may finally gain real leverage. Better engineering, better diagnostics, better research assistance, better strategic analysis, better execution.

The unsettling version is that many professional structures were built around the scarcity of exactly these skills.

If AI begins to carry a meaningful fraction of them, then the value of human work shifts upward.

The people who matter most may increasingly be those who can:

- define the right problems,
- spot high-value workflows,
- design and refine skill systems,
- set standards for evidence,
- and take responsibility when the system fails.

In other words, human value migrates toward architecture, oversight, judgment, and accountability.

That may be good. It may also be brutal in transition.

Because the same force that raises the ceiling for elite capability can compress the middle.

---

## AI Is Not Just Speeding Up Work. It Is Rewriting How Capability Compounds

This, I think, is the deepest point.

For a long time, capability compounded mainly through people.

You trained the expert. You hired the expert. You built a team around the expert. You waited years for the pattern to reproduce.

Now a different possibility is opening.

An expert may still matter just as much as before — perhaps more — but part of what they know can begin to leave the boundary of the individual and enter the boundary of the system.

When that happens, capability does not only compound through human accumulation.

It begins to compound through human-to-system transfer.

That is why I think the most important question in many industries is no longer simply, "How do we use AI?"

It is:

**Which parts of top-level skill can we make systematic, testable, and scalable without destroying the judgment that made them valuable in the first place?**

The organizations that answer that question well may build a very different kind of advantage.

---

## The Bigger Claim

So if I had to compress the argument into one sentence, it would be this:

**AI is starting to scale the most valuable thing in modern work — not information, but expert skill.**

Additive manufacturing is one clear example. CLI-based software agents are another, faster-moving one.

I doubt they will be the last.

My guess is that over the next decade, many industries will discover that the real AI race is not only a race for better models.

It is a race to build better skill infrastructure.

And that may end up being the line that separates organizations that merely use AI from organizations whose capabilities are fundamentally transformed by it.
