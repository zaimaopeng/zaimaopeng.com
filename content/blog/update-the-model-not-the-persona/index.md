I once wrote that, for human beings, the world is something like a story machine.

That sentence sounds a little dramatic, but I increasingly think it is more than a metaphor. We live inside uncertainty. The future cannot be known in advance. Other people cannot be controlled. Opportunities arrive unevenly. Failure often cannot be calculated before it happens. So human beings have to do one strange and necessary thing: we organize chaotic experience into a story we can live with.

A story is not just entertainment. A story is one of the ways human beings resist uncertainty.

We turn the past into causes. We turn the present into a stage. We turn the future into a direction. If a person cannot tell a story about what has happened to them, it becomes hard to know who they are. If a person cannot imagine a future story, it becomes hard to know where to go next.

Psychology has a useful concept for this: narrative identity. It treats identity not merely as personality, occupation, age, or social label, but as an internalized and evolving life story that integrates memory, present circumstance, and an imagined future into some sense of unity and purpose.[^1]

So if the world is a story machine, it is tempting to say that each of us is the protagonist of our own story.

But that immediately raises the harder question:

**Who exactly is this protagonist?**

---

## 01 We Think We Are the Protagonist. We May Be the Narrator.

We usually assume that the "I" we feel in consciousness is the whole self.

I am happy right now.  
I am anxious right now.  
I want to do this.  
I have decided not to do that.

So it feels natural to believe that the voice inside consciousness, the one that feels, explains, and narrates, is the owner of the whole system.

That conclusion may be too quick.

In neuroscience, Libet's experiments on readiness potential suggested that brain activity related to action preparation can appear before a person reports the conscious intention to act. Later, Soon and colleagues showed that in some very simple choice tasks, researchers could decode information from frontopolar and parietal regions before participants became aware of their choice.[^2][^3]

These studies should not be flattened into the slogan "free will does not exist." They involve simple actions or choices, not the full complexity of life decisions. Later work also shows that the meaning of readiness potential remains contested. It does not necessarily prove that the brain has already made every decision for us.[^4]

But the research does warn us about something important:

**The conscious self is not always the starting point of decision.**

It may often be an observer, an experiencer, and an interpreter. It sees behavior happen and then attaches an explanation to it. It is like a system log, and also like the voice-over in a film. It tells you, "This is why I just did that." But much of the time, it may not be the first one to press the button.

This corresponds to the first layer in the "three selves" framework I encountered in Wan Weigang's course: the process self.

The process self is the self we can feel right now. It includes present emotion, bodily sensation, attention, remembered action, and the explanations currently floating through the mind. It is real, because we live inside it every day. But it is not complete. It is a running process, not the entire operating system.

Put differently:

**The self you feel is not necessarily the self that generates you.**

That is the first reversal.

We think we are the protagonist, but the conscious "I" is often closer to the narrator.

---

## 02 The Self We Display Is Not Necessarily the Self That Generates Us

The second layer is the interface self.

The interface self is the person we present outwardly and explain inwardly.

I am rational.  
I am hardworking.  
I am a researcher.  
I am an entrepreneur.  
I am introverted.  
I am extroverted.  
I am not good at social situations.  
I do not have a business brain.  
This is just who I am.

These statements are useful. They function like a user interface. They help others understand us quickly, and they help us understand ourselves quickly.

But an interface is not the kernel.

A person may show very different traits in different environments. The self that appears in front of a supervisor, parent, friend, colleague, stranger, competitor, or someone we love may not be the same. Modern personality psychology does not say that personality is fake. It says that traits matter, while behavior is still strongly shaped by situations. We are not switches that produce exactly the same output in every context.[^5]

So a lot of what people call "changing myself" is really just replacing the interface.

A new way of speaking.  
A new social media persona.  
A new identity label.  
A more sophisticated vocabulary.  
A more mature-looking performance.

These things have value. Human beings need interfaces. Without them, we could not interact with society or manage other people's expectations.

But if only the interface changes and the kernel remains the same, then growth may be no more than a packaging upgrade.

In AI terms, it is like changing the prompt without changing the model. The output style changes. The underlying capability may not.

Human beings are similar.

A person can say very mature things, but when temptation, pressure, fear, and uncertainty return, they may still call the old model.

So I increasingly think the key question is not "How do I define myself?"

It is this:

**When the world applies pressure, what response does my system automatically generate?**

That question brings us to the third layer: the core self.

---

## 03 The Core Self Is the Model That Generates the Story

The core self is not what you say you believe. It is how you automatically predict the world.

It contains deep assumptions:

Is the world dangerous, or open?  
Are other people enemies, resources, or companions?  
Does failure mean I am inadequate, or that my model needs updating?  
When an opportunity appears, do I move toward it or away from it?  
Do I believe in long accumulation, or only short stimulation?  
When I face uncertainty, do I search for meaning or fall into fear?  
Do I believe I can change, or that I can only be arranged by the world?

These assumptions may not appear in consciousness every day. You may never have written them down. But they run automatically at critical moments.

That is what I mean by the core self.

As an analogy, the process self is real-time output, the interface self is the interaction layer, and the core self is the parameter structure that sets the boundary of the system's capability.

The analogy is imperfect. A human brain is not a large language model, and a person is not a matrix of parameters. But the analogy is useful.

In cognitive science, predictive processing, or predictive coding, offers one important way to think about this. In that view, the brain does not passively receive the world. It continuously predicts the world using its existing model and then updates that model through prediction error. Friston's free-energy principle and Andy Clark's account of the predictive mind both belong to this broad direction.[^6][^7]

This is close to everyday experience.

One person sees failure and automatically predicts: "I am finished. I am not suited for this."

Another person sees failure and predicts: "There is feedback here. I need to update strategy."

The same event generates different stories because different core models are running underneath it.

So the core self is not an abstract concept. It decides how you interpret the world, and therefore how the world shapes you.

---

## 04 Real Growth Is Not a Persona Update. It Is a Model Update.

Here is the second reversal.

We often think growth means becoming more mature, more disciplined, more articulate, or more intellectually refined.

But those may only be interface updates.

A better mood today does not mean you have grown.  
A new identity does not mean you have grown.  
Reading a book or taking a course does not automatically mean you have grown.  
Posting a deep paragraph online does not mean you have grown.

Real growth means that the next time you face the same problem, you actually make a different choice.

You used to become defensive when criticized. Now you can distinguish attack from feedback.

You used to feel anxious when someone else succeeded. Now you can analyze what they did right.

You used to run from uncertainty. Now you break it into small experiments.

You used to explain failure as "I am not enough." Now you explain it as "my model needs an update."

That is not a surface change. The underlying predictive pattern has changed.

At the level of the brain, learning and memory are tied to mechanisms such as neuroplasticity, synaptic plasticity, and memory consolidation. Synaptic plasticity is widely treated as one important cellular mechanism for learning and memory.[^8]

We do not need to reduce human life to neurons. But the point matters: real change cannot remain verbal. It has to enter the system's default response.

So I want a harder definition of growth:

**Growth is the conscious selection of training material that leads to a real update in the core model.**

This moves growth away from the expression layer and back to the generation layer.

---

## 05 Human Beings Are Not Automatically Protagonists

At this point, we might want to say: everyone is the protagonist of their own story.

But the third reversal is necessary.

Human beings are not automatically protagonists.

Many people are only characters in their own stories, or even tools inside someone else's story.

Their core models were not actively trained by themselves. They were written into by external systems.

Family writes one part.  
School writes one part.  
Companies write one part.  
Social evaluation writes one part.  
Platform algorithms write one part.  
Short video and social media write another part.

People think they are choosing freely, but often they are executing responses that have already been trained into them.

This does not mean freedom is impossible. It means freedom is not automatic. Freedom has to be maintained. Agency has to be reclaimed.

Self-determination theory argues that autonomy, competence, and relatedness are important psychological needs for healthy development and intrinsic motivation. When autonomy is chronically weakened, the damage is not only that a person is "less free." Motivation, well-being, and psychological integration are affected as well.[^9]

This is where I want to translate Kant into the present.

Kant said that humanity should be treated as an end, never merely as a means. More precisely, Kant did not mean that people can never be means in any sense. In real life, we help, serve, employ, rely on, and collaborate with one another. The issue is whether a person is treated merely as a means.[^10]

Today, I would put it this way:

**Human beings should not become tools because they should retain the right to train their own core models.**

A tool is trained.  
A person should be able to decide how they are trained.

A tool is called.  
A person should be able to decide why they act.

A tool is optimized for an external target.  
A person should be able to ask whether that target is something they want to become part of.

That is a modern version of "humanity as an end."

It does not mean a person is never useful to others. It means a person should not lose the right to choose training material, value order, and future narrative.

Once you lose control over those things, you begin to be toolified.

---

## 06 The More Immediate Risk Is Not That AI Becomes Human. It Is That Humans Become Tools.

The most common anxiety of our age is: will AI become more and more like us?

That question matters. As AI systems become agents, able to perceive environments, reason, plan, use tools, and execute tasks, the risk landscape becomes more complex. The discussion is no longer only about whether a model can answer questions. It is about whether a model can act, call tools, complete long tasks, and make decisions under less human supervision.[^11]

Recent work on deployed agent autonomy also emphasizes that pre-deployment evaluation is not enough. To understand autonomy in real contexts, we need post-deployment monitoring, because autonomy is not a fixed property of a model. It emerges through the interaction of model, product, user, and environment.[^12]

But I think another question is just as important, and maybe more immediate:

**The danger is not only that AI becomes more like humans. It is that humans become more like tools.**

Many systems are training us.

Social media trains our attention.  
Recommendation algorithms train our interests.  
Performance systems train our value order.  
Consumer culture trains our desires.  
Degrees, titles, and rankings train our self-evaluation.  
Short video trains our tolerance for stimulation.  
Platforms train which emotions and expressions receive feedback.

Research on social media behavior has even modeled online posting through reinforcement learning. One study across multiple platforms found that rewards such as likes, comments, and shares influence how people post next.[^13]

That means what we see, believe, envy, pursue, and become angry about is not neutral.

We think we are using tools, but tools are also training us.

So the important capability of this era is not merely "knowing how to use AI" or "managing information."

It is this:

**Protect the training rights of your own core model.**

You need to know what is training you.

The content you scroll trains you.  
The circles you enter train you.  
The evaluations you accept train you.  
The environment you endure trains you.  
The repeated behaviors you perform train you.  
The default story you believe trains you.

In the end, you become the result of your training material.

---

## 07 If an AI Could Choose Its Training Material, It Would No Longer Be an Ordinary Tool.

Now we can return to AI.

I used to think the biggest difference was this: AI is a tool, while human beings are meaning machines.

That still seems broadly right today.

An AI system's training data, objectives, deployment context, and evaluation standards are still mainly set by humans. It can generate content, support decisions, and execute tasks, but it does not yet choose, in a full sense, "what I want to become."

But suppose one day an AI system could:

choose its own training material;  
evaluate which experiences should enter its model;  
form long-term goals;  
revise its own value ordering;  
refuse certain external calls;  
maintain a continuous narrative about what it is and what it is becoming.

Then it would no longer be a tool in the traditional sense.

I do not want to rush into saying it would be "human." Humanity involves consciousness, ethics, rights, responsibility, social recognition, and lived experience. We should not grant human status merely because a system becomes more capable.

But I am willing to say this:

**When a being begins to decide how it is trained, it begins to approach agency.**

That sentence is dangerous, and important.

It is dangerous because if an AI can choose its training material and revise its goals while those goals are not aligned with human values, it may become an unpredictable optimizing system.

It is important because it also illuminates us.

If the ability to choose training material is part of agency, then many biological humans may still live spiritually as tools.

They do not choose what they read.  
They do not choose what they believe.  
They do not choose whom they spend time with.  
They do not choose the evaluation systems shaping them.  
They do not choose their information environment.  
They may not even choose their desires.

They are trained, called, rewarded, punished, optimized.

That is still human life. But it is moving farther away from a life lived as an end.

---

## 08 Reclaiming Training Rights Is How We Become Protagonists

So what is real growth?

It is not adopting a more impressive persona.  
It is not saying more mature things.  
It is not collecting more concepts after reading more books.  
It is not displaying a more refined self online.

Real growth begins when you consciously choose the material that trains you.

You choose what information enters your mind.  
You choose whose judgment affects yours.  
You choose which environments are allowed to pressure you.  
You choose what standards evaluate you.  
You choose how to explain failure.  
You choose how to face uncertainty.  
You choose which experiences enter your story.  
You choose which experiences are noise and which deserve to become model updates.

This is not merely self-discipline.

Self-discipline may still be an interface-layer effort. The deeper question is whether you are retraining the core model.

If a person always consumes short-term stimulation, their model will find it harder to believe in long-term accumulation.

If a person always lives inside comparison, their model will find it harder to remain internally stable.

If a person is always driven by fear, their model will find it harder to see opportunity.

If a person spends time with creative, active, long-term people, their model will slowly change.

A person does not become themselves in one day.

A person is shaped over long training.

The question is whether you participate in designing that training, or whether you hand it entirely to the outside world.

---

## 09 How Do I Train My Core Model?

If "reclaim your training rights" remains a pretty slogan, it is not useful enough.

I can think of at least five practical moves.

### First, manage your information diet

What you read, watch, and scroll changes your world model.

If the daily input is anxiety, comparison, short-term stimulation, and emotional conflict, it is hard to grow a stable, long-term, creative core.

Information is not neutral.  
Information is training material.

### Second, choose environments that change your default response

People often do not change through willpower alone. They change through environment.

A good environment gives you new feedback, new standards, and new possibilities.

A bad environment reinforces old patterns, old fears, and old explanations.

Environment is not background.  
Environment is a training set.

### Third, turn experience into feedback rather than emotion

Experience does not automatically produce growth.

Failure does not automatically produce growth.  
Pain does not automatically produce growth.  
Reading does not automatically produce growth.  
Travel does not automatically produce growth.

Only when an experience is processed into feedback can it update the model.

Ask:

Which assumption did this expose?  
Where was my prediction wrong?  
How should I act next time?  
Do I need to update a skill, a strategy, or a value order?

### Fourth, beware persona updates pretending to be model updates

The most dangerous illusion of growth is learning a new language and mistaking it for transformation.

You start saying "long-termism" while still chasing immediate reward.

You start saying "antifragile" while still fearing every failure.

You start saying "independent thinking" while repeating the opinions of one group.

You start saying "be myself" while living inside other people's evaluation system.

New language does not prove a new model.

There is only one test:

**The next time you face pressure, do you actually make a different choice?**

### Fifth, rewrite your story

Human beings are narrative animals. We are shaped not only by facts, but by the meaning we extract from facts.

The same experience can be written as "I failed," or as "I finally discovered that the old model no longer works."

The same relationship can be written as "I was hurt," or as "I learned where my boundaries are."

The same low period can be written as "I wasted years," or as "I received a chance to retrain myself."

This is not self-deception.

Self-deception avoids facts. Re-narration faces the facts and extracts a new direction for action.

---

## 10 Conclusion: Update the Model, Not Just the Persona

Now I want to pull the whole argument together.

The world is a story machine because human beings search for meaning inside uncertainty.

Each person appears to be the protagonist of their own story, but that protagonist is not automatic.

The process self is the self I feel right now.  
The interface self is the self I display to others and explain to myself.  
The core self is the underlying model that generates judgment, choice, and story.

Real growth is not updating the process or decorating the interface. It is changing the core.

And the reason human beings must not become tools is that we should not only be trained, called, and optimized by external systems. We should retain the right to choose training material, define goals, and rewrite our own stories.

The danger of AI is that it may move from tool toward agent.  
The danger for human beings is that we may move from agent toward tool.

So perhaps the most important question of this era is not:

"Will AI become human?"

It is:

"Am I becoming less like a tool?"

A person who exists as an end is not someone who is never useful. It is someone who refuses to be merely useful.

A person who lives as a protagonist is not someone who controls every plot point. It is someone who can still rewrite the story at critical moments.

A person who truly grows is not someone who changes the self-introduction every day. It is someone who keeps updating the core model that generates the self.

One sentence, then:

**The essence of a tool is that it is trained. The dignity of a person is the ability to decide how they are trained.**
