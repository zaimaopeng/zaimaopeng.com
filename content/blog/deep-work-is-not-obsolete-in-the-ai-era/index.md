If you've been using a lot of AI tools lately and somehow feel busier than before, you're probably not imagining it.

Most people's first intuition about AI is relief. You hand repetitive work to an agent, and life should get lighter. At the very least, you expect to spend less time stuck in low-value tasks. But in practice, that is often not what happens. AI does help you finish some things. It also makes it much easier to start many more things. A task moves forward a little, and suddenly three adjacent ideas feel worth exploring too. A rough framework appears, and now it feels natural to spin up another system, another draft, another experiment. The result is not fewer tasks. The result is more threads.

I've felt this more and more in my own work. I used to think three concurrent projects was about my natural limit. After I started using OpenClaw heavily, I expanded to nine, then twelve. Not because I suddenly became twice as capable, but because starting became absurdly easy. An idea appears, and I can immediately ask an agent to run with it. A vague direction starts to take shape, and within minutes it can become tasks, outlines, and rough drafts. But the fact that starting is easier does not mean finishing is easier.

Lately I've heard a certain argument floating around: in the AI era, maybe the kind of deep work people used to praise is no longer that important. Maybe the people who will thrive are the ones who are naturally more scattered, faster at associating ideas, more jumpy in their attention, almost ADHD-like in the way they move across possibilities.

I think that argument is dangerous.

Not because it is entirely wrong, but because it is only half right.

AI does amplify the value of divergence, exploration, and cheap experimentation. Many ideas that used to be expensive to test can now be pushed forward at very low cost. The first 60, 70, even 80 percent of the work is getting cheaper. You can gather information faster, draft faster, compare options faster, and turn a vague intuition into something that at least looks promising much faster than before.

But many people make a leap from “exploration is easier” to “deep work matters less.”

That leap is far too fast.

Because exploration is not delivery. Divergence is not completion. The easier the first 80 percent becomes, the more important the final 10 percent becomes. What has changed in the AI era is not that deep work disappeared. It is that the role of deep work has moved. It no longer shows up primarily as “can you personally drag the entire process forward from beginning to end with raw concentration?” It increasingly shows up as something else: can you complete the last stretch of convergence, the part that is hardest, most energy-intensive, and most decisive for quality?

That is the point I actually want to defend.

When people used to talk about deep work, they were usually describing a broad, integrated capacity: staying focused for long stretches, pushing through complexity, and carrying difficult work from start to finish. Back when tools were weak, experimentation was expensive, and starting was slow, deep work was the main engine of the whole production chain. Without it, many things simply could not begin, and they certainly could not finish.

AI changes that structure.

A lot of work is now splitting into two phases. The first is exploratory: framing the question, searching, sketching, outlining, generating options, comparing directions. The second is convergent: choosing, cutting, fixing the shape, integrating, polishing, and delivering. The first phase is becoming much easier. The second is not becoming easier at the same rate.

That is why I increasingly think the first thing AI amplifies is not our ability to complete work. It amplifies our ability to branch.

It becomes easier to begin.

Easier to push a thought into a half-finished artifact.

Easier to feel that this direction is worth testing too.

Easier to preserve multiple possibilities at once.

And once that happens, people naturally become busier. Not because the work itself is harder, but because the number of things that feel worth starting suddenly explodes.

That is not inherently bad. Divergence is good. Experimentation is good. Many genuinely new connections, topics, and directions only appear because low-cost exploration makes them possible. Without AI, some of them would never have started at all.

But there is a problem: amplifying divergence does not mean convergence happens automatically.

An agent will not decide for you which project should be stopped.

It will not cut the branches that still look plausible but are no longer worth carrying.

It will not reliably judge which direction deserves concentrated resources and which one should be abandoned early.

And it certainly will not, by default, push something from “basically done” to “actually worth delivering.”

So a lot of people think they are becoming stronger, when in reality they are only becoming more diffuse.

I gradually realized that the greatest temptation of AI is not just that it makes people more efficient. It makes people believe their capacity boundary has expanded along with their starting power.

My own attention used to be constrained by reality. I could only carry a small number of things at once. Not because I was wiser back then, but because the world itself imposed a boundary on me. Many of our limits are not internally cultivated. They are simply enforced by friction.

Once the tools get stronger, those natural boundaries start to blur. You feel like you can process more information, move more directions in parallel, and open projects you would have avoided before. On the surface that looks like an expansion of your capability. But what actually expanded, at least in my case, was my ability to start things, not my ability to carry them.

Those are not the same thing.

The tools really do let me initiate more. But they do not make my attention infinite. They do not automatically mature my judgment, patience, taste, restraint, or focus. If anything, what often expands first is not capability but appetite.

I got a more powerful tool, but my appetite did not go through the same discipline. So I wanted more. I wanted to keep more options alive. I wanted to hold on to more possibilities at once. I mistook that for growth. In many cases, it was just my sense of boundaries retreating.

That is why I care more and more about a question that sounds almost old-fashioned: where should attention actually go?

People have always said that attention should be invested in the few things that matter most. For a long time, I understood that sentence intellectually but did not fully feel its weight. Now I think it may be one of the defining abilities of the AI era. The stronger the tools become, the easier it is to misjudge your own limits. And the more badly you misjudge those limits, the easier it becomes to push everything to the same level: finished, but not excellent.

That leads to the deeper question: why is convergence so important?

Because genuine differentiation usually happens in the final stretch.

I increasingly believe that the last 10 percent of a project determines 80 percent of how its quality is perceived. It is like the last mile in logistics: often the hardest, often the most expensive, and often the part that determines whether the whole system actually delivers value. In the AI era, the earlier stages—starting, gathering, framing, organizing, outlining—are getting cheaper very quickly. But choosing, compressing, integrating, refining, and shipping are still the most energy-intensive part of the work. And they are still the part that most determines the outcome.

This is one reason so much AI content feels unsatisfying. It is not that it is unfinished. It is that it is finished in the same way as everything else.

The structure is there. The information is there. The logic is there. Sometimes it is even more complete and more efficient than what many people would produce manually. But if the final stage of convergence never happens, what you end up with is just a standard answer. It may not be bad. But it has no edge, no compression, no distinctive point of view, no sense that only you could have made it this way. It does not really rise above the mean. And if it does not rise above the mean, it does not create outsized value.

Put differently: if the last 10 percent never happens, the first 80 percent may just be a very efficient exploration process, not a meaningful delivery.

That is the real reason I disagree with the claim that deep work matters less in the AI era. Yes, the threshold for much of the old form of deep work is dropping. Many parts that once required humans to grind through complexity for hours can now be scaffolded by agents. But the final 10 percent—the part that determines whether a project becomes a project, whether a piece becomes a piece, whether a deliverable is more than just another average answer—still requires deep work.

And in some ways, it matters more than before.

Because in a world where almost everyone can get to 70 or 80 percent much faster, the only place left to create meaningful separation is the final stretch of convergence.

So my current view is almost the opposite of the fashionable claim: deep work has not disappeared in the AI era. It has been pushed toward the end.

It no longer shows up mainly as the ability to drag the whole chain yourself from zero to one hundred. It shows up as the ability to choose, among many AI-assisted 70-point and 80-point half-finished things, the one that is actually worth finishing—then keep compressing, refining, and cutting until it truly takes shape.

That is also why I have started to rethink the importance of cultivation.

We used to talk about deliberate practice as a way of training ourselves. That still matters. But now the object of training is changing. You are not only training yourself to converge. You are also training your agents to converge with you. Because an agent's default tendency is not to impose boundaries. Its default tendency is to expand. It is good at generating options, spreading possibilities, and nudging things forward a little. Without a human actively setting limits, priorities, and endpoints, it will gladly follow your own tendency to sprawl.

That means one of the genuinely scarce abilities of the future may not be “knowing how to use AI.” It may be the ability to make AI submit to your boundaries instead of letting your attention submit to AI-amplified desire.

That is why I push back against the claim that deep work no longer matters. Yes, divergence will become more valuable. Yes, experimentation will become cheaper. Yes, starting will become faster. But those things are still just exploration. Exploration is not value by itself. Divergence is not value by itself. Value still depends on whether you can pull the possibilities back together and compress them into a small number of things actually worth finishing.

AI will make it easier for more people to reach the first 80 percent.

But the last 10 percent will still require deep work.

And in an era overflowing with homogenized completion, it may become even more valuable than it used to be.

So my view now is that the most important skill in the AI era is not doing more. It is knowing what matters most. It is not starting more projects. It is knowing which few projects deserve to be finished. It is not using agents to push everything a little further. It is holding your boundary and forcing attention back onto the small number of things that actually deserve completion.

Divergence is good.

Exploration is good.

AI amplifying those capacities is also good.

But without the final stage of deep convergence, they may simply lead people into a more efficient form of distraction, rather than a more valuable form of completion.
